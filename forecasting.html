Forecasting

The forecast that’s always low

A campus café forecasts how many iced lattes it will sell each week. And every single week, the forecast comes in too low. Is that just bad luck, or is something wrong with the forecast? Pause, and decide.

Exponential smoothing

The café uses exponential smoothing. The new forecast equals the old forecast, plus alpha times the last error. Alpha is the smoothing constant. Here it is 0.5, so each new forecast closes half of the gap.

Week by week

In week one, the forecast was 40, and the café sold 48. The error is 8. Half of 8 is 4, so the next forecast is 44. But sales climbed to 52. The error is 8 again. Each week the forecast steps up by 4, and so do sales. The forecast never catches up.

MAD: how big are the errors?

Now let’s measure it. The mean absolute deviation, or MAD, is the average size of the errors. Five errors of 8 give a MAD of 8. But MAD ignores direction. A forecast that is 8 too low, then 8 too high, has exactly the same MAD.

Tracking signal: which way do they lean?

To see direction, add up the errors and keep their signs. Five errors of plus 8 add up to 40. The tracking signal divides that sum by MAD. 40 divided by 8 is 5. The control limits are plus and minus 4, so 5 is out of bounds. This forecast is biased. Simple smoothing lags behind a trend. For the forecast that swings both ways, the errors cancel. The sum is 8, and the tracking signal is only 1.

What to remember

MAD tells you how big the errors are. The tracking signal tells you whether they lean one way. When it leaves the limits, fix the model. Raise alpha, or add a trend.

The idea to keep

MAD tells you how big the errors are. The tracking signal tells you whether they lean one way.

Two forecasts can share the same MAD. Only the one whose errors pile up on one side is biased, and that is the one to fix.

Check your understanding

Answer one question, then experiment with the smoothing constant yourself.

Is this forecast biased?

Over five weeks a forecast’s errors (actual − forecast) are −6, −6, −6, −6 and −6. The control limits are ±4. What do MAD and the tracking signal tell you?

Show the worked answer

MAD = 30 ÷ 5 = 6. The sum of errors is −30, so the tracking signal is −30 ÷ 6 = −5. That is outside ±4, so the forecast is biased. The errors are negative, which means actual sales keep landing below the forecast.
